How to tailor your résumé to a job description

A generic résumé competes with hundreds of others on luck. A tailored one answers the question the posting is asking. Here is a method that takes about twenty minutes by hand, and the part of it CareerPilot does for you in two.

Why tailoring works

Two readers see your résumé, and both reward tailoring. The first is software: most medium and large employers run applications through an applicant tracking system (ATS) that searches for the skills and titles in the posting and ranks candidates by how well they match. The second is a person who spends well under a minute on the first pass, scanning the summary, the most recent title and a few bullets for the words they were told to look for. A résumé that uses the posting's own vocabulary, in the places both readers look first, gets through both filters more often.

Tailoring is not rewriting your history. Employers, dates, titles and degrees stay exactly as they are. What changes is emphasis and wording: which achievements come first, which skills are named, and how the summary frames you for this role.

The six-step method

  1. Read the posting twice and mark what repeats

    The first time, read for the role: what will this person actually do all day? The second time, mark every skill, tool, responsibility and qualifier that appears more than once or sits in the first three bullets of the requirements. You will usually end up with eight to twelve terms. Note the exact spelling the posting uses ("Kubernetes" or "K8s", "stakeholder management" or "cross-functional leadership").

  2. Map each term to something you did

    For every term, write one line of evidence from your own experience: a project, a number, a responsibility. Be honest about gaps; a term with no evidence stays out of the résumé. This map is the raw material for everything that follows, and it is also your interview preparation.

  3. Rewrite the summary for this role

    Three or four lines at the top: who you are in the posting's words, the two or three strongest proofs from your map, and the direction you are heading that matches this job. Drop anything the posting does not care about, however proud of it you are.

  4. Rebuild the skills section in the posting's order

    List the matched terms first, grouped sensibly (languages, platforms, methods), using the posting's spelling. Leave out skills you cannot discuss in an interview. A skills section is a promise, not a wish list.

  5. Re-order and rewrite the bullets

    Under each role, move the bullets that support your map to the top and rewrite them as action, scope and result: what you did, at what scale, with what outcome, ideally with a number. Mirror the posting's verbs where they fit. Bullets for duties the posting never mentions can shrink or go.

  6. Keep it single-column and plain

    Use a single-column layout, standard section headings (Experience, Education, Skills), real text rather than tables or text boxes, and a common font. Decorative two-column designs look good to people and confuse parsers; the plain version is the one that gets read.

Mistakes that undo the work

  • Keyword stuffing. A skills section with forty terms, or bullets that repeat the posting word for word, reads as spam to people and often to software.
  • Changing facts. Inflating a title or stretching dates is discovered in reference checks and ends the process. Tailor wording, never history.
  • One résumé for every posting of the same title. Two "Senior Backend Engineer" roles can want different things; the map is per posting.
  • A cover letter that disagrees with the résumé. Both documents must tell the same story; see how to write a cover letter that matches the job.

Checklist before you send

  • The summary names the role and the two or three proofs the posting cares about most.
  • Eight to twelve posting terms appear naturally, in the posting's spelling.
  • The top bullet under each recent role supports this posting.
  • Every claim is something you can talk about for five minutes.
  • Single column, standard headings, exported as PDF unless the form asks for Word.
