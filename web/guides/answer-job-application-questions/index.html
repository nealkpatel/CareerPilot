How to answer job application questions

The free-text boxes on an application form are read by the same people who read your résumé, often first. Short, specific answers that agree with your documents move you forward; long, generic ones get skimmed and forgotten.

Rules that apply to every question

  • Answer the question asked, in the first sentence. Context, if any, comes after.
  • Be specific. One named project, number or decision is worth three adjectives.
  • Stay consistent. Titles, dates, technologies and claims must match the résumé and cover letter exactly. Screeners compare.
  • Respect the box. If the field is small, two or three sentences. If it allows 2,000 characters, you still do not owe them 2,000 characters.
  • Tell the truth, especially on authorisation, availability, education and anything a background check can test.

The questions you will meet most

"Why do you want to work here?"

One specific, true reason about the company or product, then one sentence connecting it to what you want to do next. "I have used your API in two previous roles and watched the developer experience improve release by release; I want to work on the team that owns it." Avoid praise you could paste into any company's form.

"Why are you a good fit for this role?"

Pick the two requirements the posting stresses most and give one proof for each, in the posting's terms. This is your tailored résumé's summary, rewritten as a direct answer.

"Describe a challenge you overcame" (and other behavioural prompts)

Use the STAR shape without announcing it: the situation in one sentence, the task you owned, the actions you took (most of the answer), the result with a number if possible. Choose a story that uses the skills the posting asks for, and keep it under 150 words.

"What are your salary expectations?"

Give a range whose bottom you would accept, based on the posting's stated range if there is one and on market data if not, and add that you are open to discussing the full package. A number is usually better than "negotiable", which can be read as either unprepared or expensive.

"When can you start?" and "Are you willing to relocate / work on-site?"

State the facts plainly: notice period, earliest date, the locations and patterns you accept. A clear "no" on relocation saves everyone a week; a vague "maybe" tends to be read as "no" anyway.

"Are you authorised to work in …? Will you need sponsorship?"

Answer exactly and truthfully. If sponsorship is needed, say so and, if useful, what kind and when. Many employers can sponsor and simply need to plan; none can fix a misstatement later.

"Is there anything else you would like us to know?"

Use it only if there is something: a gap explained in one sentence, a portfolio link, a relevant certification in progress. Otherwise, one sentence that repeats your availability is enough. An empty box is better than a filler paragraph.

Keeping answers consistent across many applications

If you apply to many roles, keep a small bank of proven answers (your two or three best stories, your authorisation sentence, your salary approach) and adapt the first sentence of each to the posting. What must change every time is the "why here" answer and the choice of which proofs lead; what should never change is the facts.
