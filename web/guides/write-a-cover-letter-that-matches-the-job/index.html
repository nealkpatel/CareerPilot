How to write a cover letter that matches the job

Many cover letters are never read; the ones that are read decide close calls. A good letter takes the posting seriously, says three specific things, and never contradicts the résumé next to it.

What a cover letter is for

The résumé proves you can do the job. The letter answers the two questions a résumé cannot: why this role, at this company, now, and what you would bring in your own words. It is also the only place to address something the résumé makes a reader wonder about: a career change, a gap, a relocation. If you have nothing to say on those points, keep the letter short rather than padding it.

A structure that fits on one screen

  1. Opening: the match, in one sentence

    Name the role and the single strongest reason you fit it, with a proof: "I have spent four years building the kind of event-driven billing systems your Platform team is hiring for, most recently at Acme, where my team cut invoice errors by 60%." Not "I am writing to apply for the position of…", which tells the reader nothing they did not know.

  2. Why you: two or three proofs from the posting's priorities

    Take the two or three requirements the posting repeats and give one concrete example for each, with scope and result. Use the posting's own terms. These should be the same achievements your tailored résumé puts first; the letter expands them into a sentence or two of context the résumé has no room for.

  3. Why them: one specific, true reason

    A product you have used, a technical decision they wrote about, a market they are entering that you know. One real sentence beats a paragraph of praise. If you cannot find a specific reason, say what about the role itself attracts you and move on.

  4. Close: availability and a plain ask

    When you can start, anything practical (location, visa status if relevant and favourable), and a one-line request for a conversation. Sign with your name and contact details.

Aim for 250–350 words. Recruiters read letters on phones between other tasks; a letter that ends before they scroll gets finished.

Proof, not adjectives

"Passionate", "detail-oriented" and "results-driven" are claims the reader has seen a thousand times and cannot verify. A number, a named system, a before-and-after sentence can be verified, and that is what makes a reader trust the rest. Every paragraph should contain at least one thing that could only be written by you.

The letter and the résumé must agree

The fastest way to lose a close call is a letter that mentions a project the résumé does not, dates that differ by a year, or a title the résumé words differently. Write the letter after the résumé is tailored, from the same facts, and reuse its wording for titles, employers and technologies. When you edit one, re-read the other.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Repeating the résumé bullet by bullet instead of adding context.
  • Opening with your life story; the match goes first.
  • A generic letter with the company name swapped in, which the second paragraph gives away.
  • Apologising for gaps instead of stating the fact and what you did meanwhile.
  • Jokes, unusual formatting and photos; the letter is read quickly by people who did not ask for either.
